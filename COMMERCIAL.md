## What the community license (AGPL-3.0-or-later) already allows, for free

- Personal use, on your own machine.
- Use inside one company's internal network, including modifying it for that use.
  AGPL obligations attach to *distributing* the software or *offering it to
  others over a network* — not to running it privately.
- Reading, forking and learning from the source.

If that is all you need, you never have to talk to anyone.

## When you need a commercial license

- **Embedding it in a closed-source product** that you ship to customers
  (firmware, a desktop app, an appliance, a bundled installer).
- **White-labelling / OEM**: shipping it under your brand as part of your
  product.
- **Offering it as a hosted service** where you would otherwise have to hand
  your users the complete corresponding source of your modified version
  (AGPL §13).
- **Wanting a support contract**: response times, private patches, an escalation
  path.

## What a commercial license gives you

- The right to use, modify and distribute the software in the situations above
  **without AGPL obligations** on your own code.
- A written statement of the scope, so your legal review has something to file.
- Optional support terms (not 7×24; stated per contract).

It does **not** buy features. The community build is the full build — there is no
"enterprise edition" with more functionality behind a paywall. That is deliberate:
a crippled free tier is how open-core projects get forked.

## Pricing units (not a price list)

- **OEM / preinstalled**: per shipped device, or an annual platform license.
- **Closed-source embedding**: annual fee per product line.
- **Hosted service**: per seat, or a revenue share — one of the two, with the
  audit method written down.
- **Support**: annual, with stated response windows.

Ask for a quote with: what you are shipping, how many devices/seats, whether the
software is visible to your users, and whether you need support.

## Prior versions

Releases ≤ 0.6.0 remain MIT forever. A commercial license is not required to use
them, and no one — including the maintainer — can revoke that.
